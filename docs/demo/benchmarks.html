SDG 11.6.2 · undata/sdg/EN_ATM_PM25

Fine particulate matter

Tier 1 · city aggregate Proxy

Where NYC sits

6.60 µg/m³ in 2019, against 7.57 for the US city aggregate — and the gap has widened every year since 2016.

Why this one is Tier 1

The UN publishes a degree-of-urbanisation slice, so this compares NYC to the city parts of the US rather than to the country. Only five indicators have it.

Where NYC sits in the world · 2019

#5 of 186

Only four countries' cities breathe cleaner air than New York's — Iceland, Guadeloupe, Finland and Estonia. This is the one comparison here that is like-for-like: Tier 1 means NYC is measured against other countries' city aggregates, not against whole nations.

Read with this

Units agree (µg/m³) and both are annual means, but the methods differ: NYC's figure comes from NYCCAS ground monitors, the UN's is a population-weighted modelled estimate. Comparable in level, not interchangeable.

SDG 16.1.1 · undata/sdg/VC_IHR_PSRC

Intentional homicide

Tier 3 · national Proxy

The crossing

NYC ran above the national rate until 2013, then below it — and stayed below through the 2021 spike. Invisible from either dataset alone.

The 2020 gap is deliberate

There is no ACS 1-year release for 2020, so there is no denominator and no rate — on the year NYC's count jumped to 456. We left it empty.

Where NYC sits in the world · 2019

#85 of 137

Against the United States, NYC looks like a success story. Against the world it sits in the bottom half — between Pakistan and Montenegro, with 84 countries reporting a lower rate. Same number, different comparator, opposite conclusion.

Read with this

NYPD records murder & non-negligent manslaughter, which tracks the UNODC intentional-homicide definition closely. NYC publishes a count, not a rate — the denominator is Census ACS annual population. Pre-2006 rows in the NYPD file carry junk incident dates (17 murders in 1990) and are excluded.

SDG 11.6.1 · undata/sdg/EN_MWT_RCYR

Municipal waste recycled

Tier 3 · national Proxy

The naive read

NYC recycles 17.2% against 23.6% for the US in 2018 — a six-point gap, and an easy headline.

The honest read

The two numbers do not cover the same waste. The gap is real but overstated, and by an amount nobody has quantified.

Where NYC sits in the world · 2019

#38 of 64

Between Bahrain and Greece, and behind Japan and Germany. Only 63 countries report this indicator at all, so the field is thin — and the definitional caveat below applies to every one of these comparisons, not just the US one.

And one figure here is impossible. Malaysia reports 147.7% of its municipal waste recycled in 2019 — in the authoritative UN database. We left it in the data and clipped it off the axis rather than deleting it, because a silent drop is how a dataset launders its own errors. It is marked in red at the right edge.

This is the chart the whole project exists for

DSNY tonnage covers residential collection only. The UN municipal-waste definition includes commercial waste. Same units, same period, same apparent shape — and the comparison is still not apples to apples. Every automated check passes here. Only a person reading both definitions catches it.

One that ranks, but cannot track

We first marked road deaths blocked because the UN holds a single observation. That is true of a trend and irrelevant to a ranking — WHO publishes one global round, and 195 countries in one year is more than enough to place a city. The blocker was our framing.

SDG 3.6.1 · undata/sdg/SH_STA_TRAF

Road traffic deaths

Tier 3 · national Rank only

What each side holds

NYC: 11 years of rates, every fatality geocoded, updated daily. The UN: 1 point, 2021. One line, one dot — on the same axis.

NYC's own trend

3.53 in 2013 down to 2.75 by 2018, then back up to 3.51 in 2021 and 3.16 in 2024. Visible only because NYC keeps the series the UN does not.

Where NYC sits in the world · 2021

#18 of 196

NYC's streets are as safe as Spain, the Netherlands and Germany, and roughly four times safer than the United States as a whole (14.2 per 100k). That finding is invisible while the comparator is the country NYC sits in — which is the argument for this card existing at all.

Read with this

A rank is not a trend. NYC can be placed among 195 countries in 2021 and cannot be tracked against them in any other year. One with no comparator at all

The United States does not report municipal waste collected to the UN — zero observations. Ninety other countries do. So this is the case where a city can be placed among the world precisely because its own country is absent from the table.

SDG 11.6.1 · undata/sdg/EN_MWT_COLLV

Municipal waste collected

Tier 3 · national Proxy

NYC's own trend

From 453.6 kg per person in 2005 to 387.6 in 2024 — about 15% less waste per New Yorker over two decades.

No US line to draw

Not a gap in our pipeline. The UN holds 0 observations of this indicator for the United States, across every year.

Where NYC sits in the world · 2019

#41 of 91

Mid-table, between Hungary and Belarus. Read the caveat before believing it.

The caveat inverts this one

DSNY collects from residences and institutions only; commercial waste goes to private carters and never appears here. The UN definition includes commercial waste. So NYC's 397.9 kg is an undercount, its true figure is higher, and its true rank is worse than 41st — by an amount this page does not quantify.

Note the direction. The same residential-only gap makes NYC look worse on the recycling chart above and better here, because it sits in the numerator there and the numerator alone here. One definitional difference, two opposite distortions, on one page.

The US is silent on 130 indicators. Twelve of them, a city can fill.

The card above is not a curiosity. Of the 442 SDG indicators with usable data, the United States reports nothing at all for 130 — and on those, NYC vs the US is not a weaker comparison than NYC vs the world. It is the only one available, and the US is not in it.

Eleven of these twelve are proposals, not mappings

Only municipal waste collected is graded and charted on this page. The other eleven are candidates an embedding matcher proposed; on hand-read lists its precision is roughly half. Under comparability spec v0.1 a grade is a human judgment, so none of them is a mapping until a person says so — which is the same rule that keeps seven pairs off the charts below.

3 of the twelve carry a single year. That supports a level comparison and not a trend — the same finding that removed road safety as this page’s headline demo. Not one of the twelve has a single United States datapoint; each was re-checked against the country observations rather than inherited from the screening proxy.

And two the framework has no words for at all

Everything above runs city → UN: take an indicator, find the NYC series. That direction can only ever surface what the framework already asks about. Run it backwards — take every dataset 45 cities publish, find each one’s nearest indicator among all 519 named ones, and look at what is left over.

Electoral administration

The framework
0 of 519 named indicators mention an election, a vote or a turnout. Searched for election, electoral, vote, voter, voting, turnout, ballot, referendum, suffrage, polling, candidate. It counts parliamentary seats and asks whether people believe decision-making is inclusive — never how an election ran.
What the cities publish
469 datasets · 23 cities Results by polling station, electoral registers, poll-site locations. Milan’s run back to 1996. 64% sit in the bottom quarter of their own catalog by distance to any indicator.

For 35 of those election datasets, the closest concept in the entire SDG framework is municipal waste collected — the indicator charted two sections above.

Records-access requests

The framework — SDG 16.10.2
1 indicator “Countries that adopt and implement constitutional, statutory and/or policy guarantees for public access to information.” It asks whether a law exists.
What the cities publish
73 logs · 8 cities How many requests were filed, how many answered, how long it took. Whether the law works. A country can score full marks on 16.10.2 and answer nothing.

Public procurement

The framework — SDG 12.7.1
3 empty slots SG_SCP_PROCN, _HS and _LS sit in the goal tree carrying no name and zero observations for zero countries. The one adjacent indicator that does hold data asks whether a country has an action plan, and its value is 1 for all 75 countries that answer.
What the cities publish
150 datasets · 28 cities Tenders, awards, vendors, purchase orders. New York City registers 55,806 contracts worth $147bn; Portland, Oregon publishes 14,955 tenders and 9,913 contracts in OCDS going back to 2015, at least $9.7bn. None of it is counted anywhere in the framework.

This is the broadest of the three categories — 28 cities, against 23 for elections and 8 for records access — and the least concentrated: New York is only 24% of it, where Milan is 66% of the election datasets. It is the one least likely to be an artefact of who happens to publish.

Count the cities, not the datasets

Two of the three are dominated by one publisher — Milan is 66% of the election datasets, Chicago 64% of the request logs. Procurement is the exception, spread across 28 cities with no publisher above a quarter, which is why it is the sturdiest of the three. One city publishing three hundred files is a filing habit. Twenty-eight cities each publishing some is a category of municipal government, and that is the number these claims rest on.

This counts titles that carry the vocabulary, in six languages, not datasets an embedding grouped together. An earlier clustering run put records access in 15 cities; only 8 of them publish a dataset that says so, and the rest were things like Media Releases that merely clustered nearby. The smaller number is the one that survives being checked.

The seven that don't — and why

A blocked pair is not a failure of the pipeline. Recording why two numbers cannot share an axis is the product; a crosswalk showing only the easy pairs would be the thing we are building against.

IndicatorVerdictWhy it fails
Maternal mortalitySDG 3.1.1 Blocked The UN rate is per 100,000 live births, not per population — the denominator lives in a different NYC dataset. NYC also publishes pregnancy-associated deaths, a deliberately broader definition.
Child mortalitySDG 3.2.1 Context only UN counts deaths under five; NYC's series is under one. Both are raw counts, so the units "agree" — which is exactly the trap. The chart would look perfect and compare different populations.
PovertySDG 1.2.1 Context only The UN series is the international extreme-poverty line (~$2.15/day), near zero for any US city. NYC's own measure is an order of magnitude higher. Two numbers sharing a name and measuring nothing alike.
Deaths from air pollutionSDG 3.9.1 Context only UN reports a national death count from ambient air pollution; NYC reports an age-adjusted rate for ages 30+ from PM2.5 specifically. Different measure, age base and pollutant scope.
Inadequate housingSDG 11.1.1 Context only UN measures the share of urban population in slums. NYC's nearest analogue is a count of hazardous housing violations — an enforcement output that tracks inspection rates as much as conditions.
Built-up area per capitaSDG 11.7.1 No NYC source Tier 1 UN data with city-level slices across countries, 2000–2025. No NYC equivalent identified yet. Recorded so the gap stays visible rather than forgotten.
Safely managed drinking waterSDG 6.1.1 No signal Sits at ~100% for the US across the whole window, as it would for NYC. Two flat lines at 100 tell an analyst nothing. Excluded deliberately — a ceiling effect is a reason not to build a view, and that judgment belongs written down.

The inversion

We expected the UN to be the rich side and NYC the patchy one. On the city's flagship street-safety programme it is the other way round — and the consequence is not that the comparison fails, but that it only works in one direction.

New York City · Vision Zero
11 years, updated daily Every fatality geocoded, ~2.2M collision records since 2012. A trend, a seasonality, a response to policy.
UN · SDG 3.6.1
195 countries, one year A single modelled WHO round for 2021. Enough to rank the world. Not enough to watch it move.

So NYC can be placed and cannot be tracked. We can say the city sits 18th of 196, beside Spain and the Netherlands. We cannot say whether it is closing on them, because the world has one snapshot and NYC has eleven years.

That asymmetry is the argument for city data flowing toward the UN system rather than only the other way. The cities already hold the series. What is missing is the route by which it reaches the statistics everyone else compares against.

Method. 689 base indicators enumerated from the SDG goal trees via the UN Data Commons MCP and REST endpoints; 442 screened as usable, 312 of them with a US series; NYC counterparts proposed by embedding search over all 2,400 NYC Open Data datasets, then graded by hand. Comparability grades are human judgments — the harness verifies that a mapping still resolves and that units agree, and a human grade vetoes it.
